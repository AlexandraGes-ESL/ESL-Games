Reading · TOEFL style

The Personality Problem at Paws & Co.

The staff at Paws & Co. Animal Shelter had a problem. They had twelve cats, twelve very different personalities, and a waiting list of families who all wanted "a nice, friendly cat." The trouble was that not every cat agreed on what "nice" meant.

Take Cleo. She was assertive — she knew exactly what she wanted and made sure she got it. If you open a tin of tuna anywhere in the building, she appears within seconds. Some families loved her energy; others were less sure.

Then there was Biscuit — generous, patient, and completely reliable. If a new kitten arrives feeling frightened, Biscuit will sit beside them until they calm down. He never pushes. He simply waits.

The most eccentric resident was Professor Whiskers, who predicted the weather by watching dust near the radiator. He was wrong roughly 80% of the time, but this did not seem to bother him in the slightest.

The newest arrival, a shy tabby named Pixel, had not left her box in three days. The staff were considerate of her needs. If they had rushed her, she would have become even more anxious. Instead, on the fourth day, she stepped out on her own and sat in the sun for exactly eleven minutes.

By month's end, all twelve cats had homes. Curiously, the families who adopted them were rarely the ones who had asked for "friendly." Sometimes the most impulsive families chose the most laid-back cats — and somehow, it worked perfectly.

Vocabulary in Context

1. The word "assertive" (para 2) is closest in meaning to:

✅ B. "Knew what she wanted and got it" = confident, not aggressive. Some families "loved her energy" — no negative tone.
Inference

2. Mentioning Professor Whiskers was "wrong 80% of the time" mainly shows that he is:

✅ B. "Did not seem to bother him" immediately after the failure rate = charm of eccentricity, not distress.
Inference

3. Pixel sitting in the sun for "exactly eleven minutes" suggests she is:

✅ B. The precise time signals control — a shy, cautious personality moving forward in small, deliberate steps.
Main Idea

4. What is the central idea of the passage?

✅ B. The closing image — impulsive families choosing laid-back cats, "and it worked" — clinches the argument: match beats label.
Grammar · IF Conditionals

If Cats Could Talk...

You just met three real conditionals inside the reading text. Let's slow down and look at them calmly — no rush, just patterns.

Notice: the conditionals in the text are highlighted in amber. Each one uses a different type.

Type When to use Structure From the text
Zero General facts, always true if + present simple, present simple If you open tuna, she appears.
First Real / likely future if + present simple, will + verb If a kitten arrives, Biscuit will sit beside them.
Second Imaginary / unreal present if + past simple, would + verb If they rushed her, she would become anxious.

Spot the Mistake — Cat Bloggers Edition

Each sentence has one grammar mistake. Think, then click Show answer to reveal.

🐱 "If I will see tuna, I always run to the kitchen."
❌ will see → ✅ see
Zero conditional: both clauses use present simple. No "will" in the if-clause.
🐱 "If the vet would arrive early, I will be calmer."
❌ would arrive → ✅ arrives
First conditional: if-clause needs present simple, not "would."
🐱 "If I was a lion, I would be much more confident."
❌ was → ✅ were
Second conditional: use "were" for all subjects in formal/TOEFL writing — "If I were…"
🐱 "If my human opens tuna, I would always come running."
❌ would always come → ✅ always come
Zero conditional: present simple in both clauses — "would" belongs to 2nd conditional.
🐱 "If they will be more patient, Pixel would leave her box sooner."
❌ will be → ✅ were
Second conditional: if-clause needs past simple. "would" in the result clause confirms it's 2nd conditional.
🐱 "If Cleo doesn't get tuna, she would knock everything off the table."
❌ would knock → ✅ will knock
First conditional: present simple in if-clause → "will" in result clause. This is a real, likely scenario.

Homework · Reading

The Cat Who Kept Going

Read the passage at home. Answer the questions — click to check.

No one at the shelter expected much from Fig. He had arrived thin, fearful, and completely unreliable around other animals. In his first week, he hissed at the staff, knocked over his water bowl twice a day, and refused every toy they offered.

But the shelter director, a quietly patient woman named Dr. Chen, saw something different. If a cat reacts that strongly, it means it still cares, she told her team. She was not concerned by the noise. She was optimistic.

Over the following months, the staff remained consistent and supportive. They did not reward the hissing, but they never punished it either. If Fig knocked over his bowl, they simply refilled it without comment. Slowly, the hissing became less frequent. Then it stopped altogether.

By spring, Fig had become one of the most reliable cats in the shelter — calm with strangers, gentle with kittens, and completely trustworthy around food. Dr. Chen did not call it a miracle. She called it consistency.

"Resilience," she wrote in her notes, "is not the absence of difficulty. If you stay patient and supportive, difficult things often become reliable ones. Fig did not change because we forced him. He changed because we gave him time."

Vocabulary in Context

1. In paragraph 1, "unreliable" most nearly means:

✅ B. Context clues: "hissed," "knocked over," "refused" — all unpredictable behaviours, not laziness or aggression toward people.
Inference

2. Dr. Chen's statement "If a cat reacts that strongly, it means it still cares" suggests she is:

✅ B. She sees strong reaction as engagement, not a problem — a classic optimistic reframe. The text confirms: "She was not concerned… She was optimistic."
Rhetorical Purpose

3. Why does the author include "She called it consistency" in paragraph 4?

✅ C. "Not a miracle" directly dismisses luck. "Consistency" points to deliberate, repeated supportive action — reinforced in her notes at the end.
Connecting Ideas

4. The conditional "If you stay patient and supportive, difficult things often become reliable ones" is an example of:

✅ B. Present simple in both clauses + universal claim ("often become") = Zero conditional used to express a general principle, not a specific future event.
