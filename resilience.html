The Personality Problem at Paws & Co.

The staff at Paws & Co. Animal Shelter had a problem. They had twelve cats, twelve very different personalities, and a waiting list of families who all wanted "a nice, friendly cat." The trouble was that not every cat agreed on what "nice" meant.

Take Cleo. She was what the staff called assertive — she knew exactly what she wanted and she made sure she got it. If you opened a tin of tuna anywhere in the building, she appeared within seconds. Some families loved her energy. Others were less sure.

Then there was Biscuit — generous, patient, and completely reliable. If a new kitten arrived at the shelter feeling frightened, Biscuit would always be the first to sit beside them. He never pushed. He never rushed. He simply waited until they were ready.

The most eccentric resident was undoubtedly Professor Whiskers. He had developed a system for predicting the weather by watching which way the dust moved near the radiator. If the dust moved left, he would hide under the sofa — which, according to his records, meant rain was coming. He was wrong approximately eighty percent of the time, but this did not seem to bother him.

The newest arrival, a small shy tabby named Pixel, had not left her box in three days. The staff were thoughtful about her needs — they kept the lights low and spoke quietly near her space. If they had rushed her, she would have become even more anxious. Instead, on the fourth day, she walked out on her own and sat in a patch of sunlight for exactly eleven minutes.

By the end of the month, all twelve cats had found homes. The families who adopted them were not always the ones who had asked for "friendly." Sometimes the most impulsive families chose the most laid-back cats, and somehow it worked perfectly.

Why does the author most likely mention that Professor Whiskers was "wrong approximately eighty percent of the time"?

✅ C is correct. The detail about being wrong 80% of the time immediately followed by "this did not seem to bother him" highlights that Professor Whiskers is undisturbed by failure — which is exactly what makes him eccentric rather than just wrong.

Grammar

IF Conditionals

You already saw these in the text — now let's see the system behind them.

The Three Conditionals

Type When to use Formula From the text
Zero Always true facts, habits, general truths
If + Present Simple, Present Simple
 "If you open a tin of tuna, she appears within seconds."
First Real possibility in the future
If + Present Simple, will + verb
 "If a new kitten arrives, Biscuit will sit beside them."
Second Imaginary / hypothetical present or future
If + Past Simple, would + verb
 "If they had rushed her, she would have become anxious."*

* The text uses Third Conditional here — we'll cover that in a future lesson. For now, focus on Zero, First and Second.

⚠️ Easy to confuse

Zero vs First: "If I heat water, it boils." (always true) vs "If I study tonight, I'll pass the test." (this specific situation)

First vs Second: "If she calls, I'll answer." (realistic) vs "If she called, I would answer." (imaginary — she probably won't call)
