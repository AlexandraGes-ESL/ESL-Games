Chapter 01 · Reading

A Critic Confesses

Read once for the main idea. Read again and notice the bold words — they reappear in everything that follows today.

I have a confession. For years I told visitors what each painting meant — confidently, as if the artist had whispered it in my ear. People assume a critic walks into a gallery and instantly knows. The truth is messier.

A young couple once stopped beside me at a large red canvas. "It is about anger," the man said with total confidence. His partner disagreed: for her, the red was clearly a symbol of love. They were both certain. They could not both be right — or could they?

For years I believed my job was to recover the artist's intention — to dig up the "correct" meaning like an archaeologist. But the artists we study are no longer living. I cannot phone them. So whose interpretation wins: theirs, mine, or the couple's at the red canvas?

That question is far more complex than I once admitted. And admitting it, strangely, made me a better critic — not a worse one.

Glossary · Issue 04

Key Words

Carry these into your speaking today. Aim to use each one at least once.

  • intention — what the artist wanted to express or achieve.
  • interpretation — the meaning a viewer gives to a work.
  • to convey — to communicate a feeling or idea through form.
  • ambiguous — open to more than one reading; deliberately unclear.
  • to advocate (for) — to argue publicly in support of an idea.
  • complex — made of many connected parts; not simple.

Before You Watch · Video Glossary

Six Words from the Video

These come up in the talk. Read them first — listening becomes 80% easier when the key words are already in your head.

  • fallacy — a belief that seems true but is logically wrong.
  • the Intentional Fallacy — the idea that the artist's intention should not decide a work's meaning.
  • to interpret / interpretation — to decide what something means.
  • analogy — a comparison used to explain an idea (e.g. art is like a pudding).
  • flavour / to taste — used in the pudding example: the experience, not the recipe.
  • a definitive answer — one final, certain answer that ends all doubt.

Chapter 03 · Speaking Lab

The Voice

First, train the mouth — Listen & Repeat. Then, train the mind — three speaking cards of rising challenge, always with support.

A · Listen & Repeat

Two passes. Pass 1: listen and read along — get the rhythm. Pass 2: hide the text and repeat from memory. There is no penalty for peeking — accuracy first, memory second. In class: 1–3 together. Full set → homework.

01

A striking painting catches your eye.

Pronunciation: /str/ cluster · /tʃ/ in "catches" · 5 words

02

Pronunciation: listing rhythm · pause at comma · 9 words

03

Weak forms: "we" /wɪ/ · "are" /ə/ · linking · 7 words

05

Chunking: pause at comma · contraction "don't" · 11 words

07

B · The Interview

Three cards, rising challenge. 30 seconds to plan, 45 seconds to speak. Use at least one Glossary word per answer. Notes are your friend — the goal is rich ideas, not a tightrope walk.

Card 1 · Warm-up

Describe a work of art (any kind) that you cannot forget. What does it convey to you?

Use: to convey, interpretation.
Structure: name it → describe it → say why it stuck.

Card 2 · Opinion

"The artist's intention is the only thing that matters." Do you agree?

Use: intention, to advocate, ambiguous.
Take a side, then admit one weakness in it.

Card 3 · The Hard One

A child's drawing sells for $1 million. Is it art, a joke, or a scam? Defend your view.

Use: complex, value, one hedge from Ch.04.
Starters: "At first glance it seems… / One could argue… / On balance, I'd say…"
Take 30 sec to plan. Notes allowed — fluency beats perfection.

Chapter 04 · Language Tools

The Art of the Hedge

Confident speakers are not always certain — they are precise about how certain they are. Learn to soften, qualify, and signal doubt without sounding weak.

The Certainty Scale

From "100% sure" to "just a guess." Notice how the grammar changes as confidence drops.

CertaintyPhraseExample (about a painting)
High · 90–100%It is clearly… / There is no doubt that…It is clearly a portrait of grief.
Mid · 60–80%It seems to… / It appears that… / I'd say…It seems to convey loneliness.
Low · 30–50%It might… / Perhaps… / One reading could be…It might be a comment on time.
Very low · <30%I suppose… / It's hard to say, but… / If anything…I suppose the red could be anger — it's hard to say.

Rescue Phrases · Buy Yourself Time

C1 speakers don't avoid pauses — they fill them gracefully. Memorise three. Use them instead of going silent.

When you need a second to thinkWhen you can't find a word
That's a really interesting question…How can I put this…
Let me think about that for a moment.It's hard to explain, but…
There are a few ways to look at this.The word escapes me — basically, it means…
Why this matters: the difference between B2 and C1 is often not vocabulary — it's never going fully silent. A graceful filler keeps you in control.

Your Support · How to Build a Hedged Interpretation

Three Steps + One Model

Don't say "I like it." Build a real interpretation in three moves. Follow this skeleton for every painting above.

1

DESCRIBE — what you literally see

"At first glance, I notice… / The painting shows… / What stands out is…"

2

INTERPRET (with a hedge) — what it might mean

"It seems to convey… / One reading could be… / It might be a comment on…"

3

JUSTIFY — point back to the painting

"…because of the [colour / lines / light / composition]."

Full model · Van Gogh

"At first glance, I notice the swirling sky and the dark, flame-like cypress tree. It seems to convey a sense of restless energy — almost anxiety — because of the thick, curling brushstrokes and the way the tree cuts vertically through the calm field. One reading could be that it shows nature as something alive and emotional, not peaceful."

Award the red dot to your personal #1. Speak for 45 seconds using all three steps: describe → interpret with a hedge → justify.

Chapter 06 · Output

The Defense

The full TOEFL-style task. Read the prompt, plan for 60 seconds, then write — or record yourself speaking — a structured 2-minute response.

Prompt. Some people believe a work of art means only what the artist intended. Others believe meaning belongs to the viewer. Which position do you find more convincing, and why?

Your Response

Aim for: a clear position → two reasons with examples → one hedge acknowledging the other side → a confident close.

Self-Check · Rubric

Did you…

  • state a clear position in the first sentence?
  • give two reasons, each with an example?
  • use at least three Glossary words?
  • include one hedge from the Certainty Scale?
  • acknowledge the other side before closing?
  • finish with a confident final line?
